# Start With Excitement

## What It Is

Beginning each work session with whatever genuinely excites you — not saving it as a reward for getting through the obligations first. The excitement fills the cup and generates momentum that carries naturally into less energising tasks. Reversing the conventional order (hard stuff first, reward later) changes everything about how the session feels and what actually gets done.

## Why It Matters

The "eat the frog" model — do the hardest, most unpleasant thing first — depletes energy before you ever reach what matters to you. For people whose block lives at the meaning layer (not the skill or knowledge layer), the hard thing first often means running straight into the wall before any momentum has built. Starting with excitement bypasses the resistance pattern: you're already in motion, already in flow, by the time you reach the more obligatory work. Momentum is easier to continue than it is to start. Excitement is the ignition.

This is also how children work. They follow excitement from one thing to the next, for hours, without noticing time passing. Adulthood trains that out of people — but the energy source is still there. The principle is about accessing it deliberately rather than saving it as a treat.

## How to Use It

**As a coach:** When a client reports chronic inability to start, ask them to scan their to-do list and name the item that makes them feel something — anything positive. Start there. Not as permission to avoid the hard things — as a strategy to generate the state that makes everything else more possible.

**As a consultant:** In project planning, sequencing matters beyond just logic and dependencies. If a team is stuck, identify which workstream has the most genuine energy and start there — let that momentum carry into the less exciting parts.

**As a content creator:** The anti-productivity-porn angle: stop front-loading your hardest task. Start with what's alive. Let the energy compound. The brain dump before the creative session, not the admin.

## Content Angles

- You're not supposed to earn your excitement. You're supposed to use it as fuel.
- The "hard stuff first" model was designed for factory work. You're not in a factory.
- The child analogy: kids don't build momentum toward excitement — they start there and it carries them everywhere.
